## Q2 Models and ties

- Observed (p.5): Bradley–Terry with a logistic link; inverse-probability weighting of pair counts: "To avoid overweighting commonly sampled pairs, we use inverse-probability weighted (IPW) win counts, as prescribed in Chiang et al. [2024]." Elo scaling: Rating = 400·θ + 1000 (Eqn. 1, p.5).
- Observed, ties (p.4): "We treat a tie as half a win for both LLMs." and (p.7) ties carry weight 0.5 for both models; 'both bad' votes are excluded.
- Observed, subgroup fits (p.8): "For each eligible subpopulation, we fit a standard BT model and each resulting ranking is evaluated on its own user slice." Subpopulations with fewer than 50 votes are excluded (p.8); ensemble of 389 BT rankings (p.8) / 388 in Figure 2 caption (p.3).
- Observed, notational quirk (p.5): the text writes Pr(a ≻ b) with exp(·) and simultaneously defines σ(x) = (1 + 10^−x)^−1 as "the standard logistic function"; Appendix A.1 (p.14) uses 1/(1+exp(−δ)) with the 400-point Elo scale. Reader's inference: a base-e/base-10 inconsistency in exposition; immaterial to their conclusions.
- Not found: Rao–Kupper, Davidson or any explicit tie model (searched "Rao", "Davidson", "tie" — tie hits are only the half-win rule).

## Q3 Adequacy tests and results

- Observed: no formal goodness-of-fit, lack-of-fit, likelihood-ratio, saturated-model, transitivity, cycle/triad, or heterogeneity hypothesis test appears (see Not found). What the paper does report are descriptive diagnostics of the global BT fit on the training votes:
  1. Vote cancellation (p.2): "we show that 72.28% of decisive non-tied votes (across ∼ 89k in the entire data) are actually ‘canceled’ 6 by each other." (footnote 6 defines cancellation as an opposing decisive vote on the same model pair).
  2. In-sample predictive coverage (p.8): "The global BT ranking leaves a large fraction of individual votes poorly explained, and predicts the winner of only ∼ 11% of all votes correctly with a > 70% confidence." The authors flag that these are training votes (p.2, footnote 7): "Note that these are not unseen/test votes – these were used to train the global BT ranking (see Section 4)."
  3. Small Elo spread / near-random win probabilities (p.1): "indistinguishable (pairwise win probabilities are at most 0.53 within the top 50,".
  4. Spread of subgroup BT fits versus random and hourly partitions (Figure 1, p.2): "Language, language-family, and their task interactions induce substantially larger spreads than random, hourly, task-only, or global rankings." and (Figure 6, p.18): "rankings exhibit substantially more mass near 0 and 1 than random or hour-based baselines".
  5. In-group log loss and winner probability of 388 subgroup fits vs global (Figure 2, p.3): "Language- and family-based rankings consistently assign sharper winner probabilities and lower log loss than the global ranking, while task-specific rankings remain nonrepresentative, similar to the global baseline."
  6. Connectivity/identifiability example (p.10, footnote 10): "The BT model nonetheless assigns scores to all four and induces a total ordering Llama-4ME > o3 > Nova-Exp > GPT-4.1M, despite no direct comparisons supporting the relative positions of" the pairs.
- Observed, headline conclusion (p.10): "We show that global Bradley–Terry rankings on pairwise preference data from Arena are uninformative at the level of individual votes due to strong, structured heterogeneity across subpopulations."
- Reader's inference: the random- and hour-partition baselines in Figure 1/Figure 6 function as an informal permutation-style reference for heterogeneity, but no test statistic, null distribution, degrees of freedom or p-value is reported anywhere; the comparison is visual. The paper therefore does not test BT adequacy in the formal sense of the research question.

## Q4 Uncertainty and propagation

- Observed: no bootstrap or confidence intervals for ratings or ranks; the "statistically indistinguishable" claim (p.1) rests on the magnitude of fitted win probabilities (≤ 0.53), not on interval estimates. The only interval-type statements are (Figure 3, p.8) "shaded regions show approximate 95% confidence bands around the family mean, computed across languages within each family (showing correlations within language family)." and (Figure 8, p.25) "The dashed line shows an ordinary least squares fit, with the shaded region indicating the 95% confidence interval. The weak slope (-0.003) suggests that variation in" coverage is not explained by vote counts.
- Observed: the paper cites vote-dropout sensitivity rather than computing it (p.3): "Recent parallel work that also shows that these rankings are unreliable due to variance under vote dropout of <5% Huang et al. [2025a]."
- Reader's inference: neither sampling uncertainty nor misspecification is propagated into any stated rank uncertainty; the paper's remedy is a portfolio of subgroup rankings, not an inflated interval.

## Reader's inference

- This is the nearest neighbour to our question on the heterogeneity axis: it fits a global BT and 389 subgroup BT models on a public arena release and shows, descriptively, that language (and language × task) partitions produce much sharper and better-fitting rankings than the global fit, while random and hourly partitions do not. That is evidence of homogeneity violations, but it is not a formal specification test: there is no likelihood-ratio statistic against a stratified or saturated model, no tie model, no transitivity or cyclic-triad check, and no p-values.
- The paper does not quantify rank uncertainty at all, so nothing is propagated; its answer to misspecification is to replace the single ranking with a small portfolio.
- Voter and session identifiers were unavailable to them in the public release, the same constraint the research question faces; their within-user vs between-user caveat (p.10) is an explicit open point.
- The in-sample character of their coverage numbers (footnote 7) and the absence of a held-out or null-distribution comparison mean a formal LRT / saturated-model test on the same 140k release would be a genuine addition rather than a duplication.
