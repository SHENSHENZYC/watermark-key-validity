Reader's inference. The paper never conditions on a fixed deployed key and never states a null distribution of the score for one key.

Although some scoring functions allow a precise theoretical" / "guarantee on the false-positive rate—for example, the frequentist scoring functions (Supplementary Information section A.3) which provide" / "a P value—in this work we take the empirical approach described above." Abstention (p.5): "low error rates are desirable, we can use a selective prediction mechanism (Supplementary Information section C.8) to abstain on samples" / "for which the scoring function is uncertain, thus achieving the desired" / "error rates on the remaining data (Fig. 3b)." — configured for TPR 95% and FPR 1% (Extended Data Fig. 3, p.12).

Reader's inference. The threshold is defined as the empirical 1% quantile of the null scores, so the measured FPR equals the nominal 1% by construction on the development/test null set; no independent check of an a priori threshold against a nominal level is reported, and the dependence of that quantile on the key is not examined.

## Reader's inference
The Nature paper defines the key precisely, requires it for detection, and makes the seed uniform only by averaging over a uniformly random key (plus a PRF assumption). Its reported false-positive rates are empirical quantiles of null scores computed on human ELI5 answers under an unstated (presumably single) key, with no theoretical level, no statement of key count, and no guarantee for the default Bayesian scorer. The paper therefore neither claims nor tests a key-conditional false-positive rate.
