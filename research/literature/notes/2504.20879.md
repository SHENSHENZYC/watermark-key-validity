## Q2 Models and ties

## Q3 Adequacy tests and results

Observed — the BT assumptions the paper names (p.9):
- "The BT model assumes that pairwise comparisons are drawn from an unbiased sampling process, so that each model’s skill parameter is estimated independently and based on representative outcomes."
- "It also does not require all players to play each other because of transitivity of rankings—if Model A is judged to beat Model B and Model B in turn beats Model C, then Model A is estimated to outperform Model C"
- "Finally, it assumes that the comparison network is fully interconnected—that is, every model must be linked directly or indirectly through pairwise matchups."

Observed — the three violations argued, and the evidence for each:
1. Selective disclosure / best-of-N (Section 3). (p.10): "Selective disclosure violates unbiased sampling assumption of BT model." (p.10): "We show theoretically how BT assumptions are violated, which systematically inflates model rankings, distorting the leaderboard ranking." (p.12): "This violates the BT model’s assumption of unbiased sampling and alters the likelihood landscape."
   - Theory (Appendix C, order statistics; Appendix I, Gaussian extreme values): (p.67): "With the current Arena policy (n = 3 000, hence σnoise = 6.34Arena Score)" and "M = 50 =⇒ bias ≈ 6.34 2 ln 50 = 17.7 Arena Score." (the square-root sign is lost by pdftotext); (p.69): "σtrue = 20Arena Score yields bias ≈ 56Arena Score at N = 50, n = 3 000." and "Selection bias does not vanish."
   - Simulation (Figure 7): (p.13): "In our simulation, testing just 20 variants yields a notable increase of approximately 50 points in the maximum score identified, compared to the baseline scenario with no private testing." (p.66): "observing a lift of 50 when 20 non-identical private variants are tested." Note the Section 3 findings box states differently (p.10): "In Figure 7, we simulate the expected distortions to leaderboard Arena rankings and show that testing just 10 variants yields a notable increase of approximately 100 points in the maximum score identified." Figure 8 (p.13): "This simulation shows how providers can gain leaderboard advantage by evaluating several variants privately and publishing only the top-scoring one."
   - Real-world experiment (Section 3.3, p.15): "we observe notable differences in final Arena scores between identical checkpoints: 1052 (±21/22) and 1069 (±19/23). As a result, 4 models are positioned between our two identical checkpoints." Two Aya-Vision-32B variants: "(1097 (±29/25) vs. 1059 (±18/23)), with 9 models falling in between both variants on the leaderboard." (p.15): "We did not disclose that these were identical checkpoints to Chatbot Arena."
2. Transitivity under changing evaluation conditions (Section 5.1). (p.21): "In evolving task distributions, premature model removal introduces inconsistencies, breaking the BT model’s transitivity assumption and distorting rankings." Simulation: four models A–D, two task types with task-specific win matrices (Tables 7–8, p.71); (p.23): "Each of the four models participates in 1000 battles, and the resulting outcomes are used to compute initial rankings." then 1000 more battles with the task mix shifted; deprecating D after phase 1 changes every rank (Figure 14, p.24: A 1→2, B 2→1, D 3→4, C 4→3). (p.24): "Models A and D are ranked lower, and Models B and C are ranked higher than their true performance merits." (p.24): "This violates core assumptions of the BT model, namely, that rankings reflect transitive and consistently sampled matchups, ultimately compromising the validity of inferred rankings." Caveat (p.23): "If all models were continuously sampled across all points in time, the BT model would likely remain robust because every model would be evaluated on the evolving distribution of tasks."
3. Connectivity / sparsity (Section 5.2). (p.24): "As demonstrated by Ford Jr (1957), the maximum likelihood estimate does not exist if models can be partitioned into two nonempty subsets without comparisons between them or if all comparisons between the two groups are one-sided (i.e., one group always wins)." Simulation with seven models (true ratings 1450 to 1000) and 2000 battles under dense vs disconnected graphs (Figure 15, pp.25–26): "In contrast, a sparse or disconnected comparison graph results in an inaccurate estimation of the models’ skills." Deprecation count feeding this concern (p.69): "Of the 243 public models, 205 participated in an average of 10 or fewer battles during this period, based on leaderboard-stats."

Observed — what is not done:
- Formal goodness-of-fit, likelihood-ratio, saturated-model, cyclic-triad / stochastic-transitivity, or homogeneity test on the 1.8M real battles: not found. Heterogeneity across language is documented descriptively only (p.18): "A clear shift is observed: English prompt share dropped from over 80% to nearly 50%, while usage of Chinese, Russian, and Korean prompts increased significantly." (p.19): "Overall, the number of multilingual prompts on the Arena has grown by 20% over 1.5 years, from 23.9% in April 2023 to 43.5% in January 2025." No test of BT-coefficient homogeneity by language, category or voter is run.
- Headline framing (p.5): "We show that deprecation can violate key assumptions of the Bradley-Terry model (Bradley & Terry, 1952), which underpins Arena scoring, thereby reducing the reliability of the leaderboard rankings."

## Q4 Uncertainty and propagation

Observed:
- Simulation intervals (p.13): "We also employed bootstrapping to estimate the confidence intervals for the corresponding Arena Scores."
- Leaderboard intervals are quoted asymmetrically, e.g. 1052 (±21/22) (p.15). (p.43): "When the confidence intervals of two models overlap, it becomes difficult to determine which one is truly better. This uncertainty is reflected in the final ranking table, adding nuance and statistical rigor to the leaderboard"
- Closed-form noise (Appendix I, pp.66–67): Fisher information 0.25 per equal-strength battle, σ_β(n) = 2/√n, σ_Elo(n) ≈ 347.4/√n, so n = 3000 gives about 6.34 Arena points.
- Propagation of misspecification into stated rank uncertainty: not found. The violations are treated as biases of point estimates and answered with policy remedies (Section 6), e.g. (p.27): "While this sampling rule is clearly articulated in the paper, we have not seen evidence of its deployment in the current leaderboard." and (p.7): "We suggest a return to an active sampling method proposed by the arena organizers themselves (Chiang et al., 2024), which appears not to be respected or implemented in practice."

## Reader's inference

- Reader's inference: this audit names three BT assumptions (unbiased sampling, transitivity under constant conditions, connectivity) and supports each with simulations or an order-statistics argument; the only real-data experiment (identical Aya-Vision-8B checkpoints scoring 1052 and 1069) demonstrates selection and sampling noise, not lack of fit of BT to the votes.
- Reader's inference: "transitivity" here means non-stationarity of the task mix combined with deprecation causing rank inversions, not a measured cyclic-triad or stochastic-transitivity statistic; the paper does not estimate how often transitivity fails in the 1.8M real battles, nor test homogeneity across the language or category tags it has.
- Reader's inference: uncertainty is discussed only through standard BT intervals (bootstrap in simulation, leaderboard CIs, a closed-form σ ≈ 347/√n); no widening of intervals for measured misspecification is proposed.
- Observed-and-flagged inconsistency: the Section 3 findings box (p.10) states a lift of about 100 points at 10 variants, whereas the body (p.13) and Appendix I (p.66) state about 50 points at 20 variants; cite the body/appendix figures if this paper is used.
- Reader's inference on relevance: the most prominent critique of arena BT argues assumption violations without running formal specification tests on public vote data, which confirms the gap the research question targets.
