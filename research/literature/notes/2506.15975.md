## Q1 What is random; conditional or marginal

Observed.

- The word "key" denotes a user identifier, not a secret random seed. Detection seeds a pseudo-random generator from the text and the identifier (p.4): "a new seed is generated using the hash function as hg = H(xi−h , · · · , xi−1 , Ai (ξ, di )), which is then used to construct a standard uniform distribution ui ∼ U (0, 1, hg )."
- The null model (p.5): "For unwatermarked text or when the salt key does not match the generating key, the xi -th random variable in ui (denoted as uixi ) will simply follow a uniform distribution." and (p.6): "if a text is unwatermarked, all elements in the random variable vector ui (ξ) ∈ RV , drawn during detection follow a uniform distribution."
- Theorem 1 (p.6; restated p.12): "Theorem 1. Consider random variables uixi (ξ) drawn from a uniform distribution over [0, 1], where ξ = [1, . . . , K] represents the key, and K denotes the total key capacity." and "We consider the sample is watermarked if maxξ Sk (ξ) ≥ τk , where τk is a threshold parameter chosen to bound the FNR. The false positive probability is bounded as:" [bound: 1 − (1 − exp(T(τ_k(1/e − 1) + 1)))^K].
- Interpretation (p.6): "Our theory shows that as the number of tokens (T ) increases, false recognition rates decrease. However, as the number of keys K increases, the false positive bound also increases, leading to the aforementioned false detection problem."

Its proof (p.15) assumes independence of the two scores: "By the definition of joint probability for independent events, we can express the hybrid probability as the product of the individual probabilities:"

Reader's inference. The probability in Theorems 1–3 is taken over the hash-derived uniforms u, which the paper models as i.i.d. Uniform(0,1) under the null; since those uniforms are deterministic functions of the text and the identifier, the modelled randomness is effectively the text (through the hash), with the hash function and the identifier set {1,…,K} held fixed. The bounds are therefore marginal over the text-induced pseudo-randomness and there is no random secret key in the sense of the research question; a key-conditional false-positive rate is neither defined nor discussed. The statement is also an upper bound from a Chernoff argument, not an exact level.

## Q2 Fixed key / per-key / conditional vs marginal

Observed.

- The paper's central claim concerns multiplicity across identifiers, not variation across keys (p.1): "as user capacity grows, unwatermarked text is increasingly likely to be falsely detected as watermarked." and (p.2): "we demonstrate, both theoretically and empirically, that as the identity number increases, the false detection bound increases exponentially."
- Mechanism (p.3): "this involves taking the maximum score among all possible keys and comparing it with a threshold. This leads to an increased false positive rate as the key capacity grows."
- Per-sequence variation of the score motivates the Mean Rebalance variant (p.5): "Since the mean value of scores can vary for each sequence, using a fixed threshold may lead to errors."
- Empirical key-capacity finding (p.9): "In the FKE model, since it relies entirely on the maximal score of all keys in the key space, the performance decreases significantly as K increases." and "The HDW results show no significant differences for different key capacities in our HDW method. This is because the indicator is the dominant part and can ensure the FPR avoids the influence of the total key count."
- A fixed or deployed key, per-key calibration, key screening, conditional versus marginal validity, dispersion of the false-positive rate across keys: not found (searched: conditional (0), marginal (p.8, "marginal improvements" only), derandom (0), aggregat (0), deployed (p.12, "deployed responsibly"), screening (0), Type I (0), per-key (0), calibrat (0), fixed (pp.5, 7 only), nominal (0), significance (0), p-value (0)).

Reader's inference. The paper studies a union over K identifiers of per-identifier tests, so its "false detection problem" is a multiplicity effect across keys rather than dispersion of the Type I error of one key. It does, however, make the point that any claimed false-positive level is a property of the whole key set, and that the per-identifier bound (Theorem 2) is the quantity that stays stable as K grows; that is the nearest it comes to a per-key statement.

## Q3 Empirical FPR procedure

Observed.

- Setup (p.7): "Our experiments follow the same setup as Fernandez et al. [2023], using the Guanaco-7b model [Dettmers et al., 2024], an instruction fine-tuned LLaMA model [Touvron et al., 2023a], with the first 1,000 prompts from the Alpaca dataset [Taori et al., 2023]."
- Keys and samples (p.8): "A salt key represents IDs ranging from 1 to 1,000." and "We use 1000 samples mixed with watermarked and unwatermarked text to test all the methods." and "we generated 11 datasets with watermarked text ratios ranging from [0%, 10%, . . . , 90%, 100%]. We report the overall scores by averaging over watermarked ratios."
- Threshold selection is by accuracy on a development set, not by a nominal level (p.8): "We use 500 samples as the development set for hyperparameter selection and other 500 as a test set for evaluation with the chosen hyperparameters." and "we performed a grid search on τd and τk , exploring values within the range [0.02, 0.04, . . . , 7.98, 8.0] for all models on development set."
- Metric (p.8): "and False Positive Ratio (FPR), which indicates the extent of the false detection problem."
- Numbers, Table 1 (p.8, distribution-based backbone, FPR column): FKE 0.231, PKE 0.25, DW 0.109, HDW 0.092, MR 0.0917, SR 0.0858. Table 2 (p.8, FPR versus capacity K = 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000): FKE 0.178, 0.234, 0.233, 0.260, 0.258, 0.289, 0.295; HDW 0.173, 0.149, 0.12, 0.127, 0.103, 0.141, 0.095. Table 3 (p.8, Multi-bit backbone, FPR): FKE 0.120, PKE 0.175, DW 0.0178, HDW 0.0142, MR 0.0243, SR 0.0174. Table 7 (p.30, Multi-bit, K = 20 to 2000): FKE 0.0647 to 0.141; HDW 0.0426 to 0.0367.
- The FPR depends on the tuned threshold (p.24): "With an increasing watermarked text ratio, FPR also increases. This is because, at low watermark ratios, the tuned thresholds are set very high, making it unlikely for any sample to be classified as watermarked, thus eliminating false positive inferences." and (p.26): "as the sequence length grows, the FPR metric also increases; however, this does not necessarily indicate worsening false detection problems."
- Comparison of empirical FPR with a nominal level: not found (searched: nominal, significance, alpha, p-value). Number of distinct hash functions or secret keys: not stated; the hash H is fixed (p.12, "The hash function H is used to calculate the hash key.").

Reader's inference. The empirical FPRs are accuracy-tuned operating points averaged over watermark ratios, so they measure how a max-over-identifiers statistic shifts the null distribution, not calibration against a declared α; nothing in the experiments varies the secret randomness of the scheme.

## Q4 Remedies and guarantees

Observed.

- Dual Watermark (p.3): "Instead of using all tokens to encode the identification watermark, we selectively use certain tokens to encode the detection watermark. Such a method avoids performing the maximum operation for both distribution-based method and the dictionary-based method, thereby mitigating the false detection problem."
- Guarantee: Theorem 2 upper bound, "independent of the capacity K" (p.7, quoted in Q1); numerical comparison (p.23): "if K is larger than 114.7, the DW method’s bound is superior for all settings we test."

- Variants MR and SR (p.5) rebalance the identification score by its mean or by the gap to the second-largest score.

## Reader's inference

1. Validity here is a Chernoff-type upper bound on the false-positive probability, marginal over hash-derived pseudo-random uniforms that are assumed i.i.d. under the null; the "key" is an enumerated user identifier, the hash is fixed, and no secret random key appears. The paper therefore does not address the conditional-versus-marginal question for a deployed secret key.
2. Its contribution to the gate is adjacent rather than direct: it shows that when a detector takes a maximum over K identifiers, the achieved false-positive rate is a property of the whole identifier set and grows with K (Table 2: 0.178 to 0.295 for FKE), and that isolating a detection statistic that does not depend on the identifier (Theorem 2) removes that dependence. That is a multiplicity argument across keys, not an analysis of how the Type I error of one fixed key disperses around its nominal level.
3. The empirical FPRs are not compared with a nominal α (thresholds are tuned for accuracy on a development set), so the paper offers no evidence on calibration of a per-key test.
4. Most decisive quotation for the gate: p.6, "However, as the number of keys K increases, the false positive bound also increases, leading to the aforementioned false detection problem."
