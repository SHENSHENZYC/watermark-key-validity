## Q1 What is random; conditional or marginal

Observed.

- The calibration objects are watermark p-values of essays (p.6): "we use S = {s1 , . . . , sn } to refer to the original p-values returned by watermarking detection providers, indicating the strength of evidence against the null hypothesis that a piece of text is entirely human-written."
- The conformal p-value (p.6): "Given a new submission, the professor receives the watermark score s and determines whether the essay is likely to be a violation of the guidelines by computing the conformal value p" [eq. (1): û(s) = (1 + Σ 1{s_i ≤ s})/(n + 1)]. Interpretation (p.6): "This conformal p-value has a fundamentally different interpretation from the watermark p-value. It represents: “What proportion of essays with permitted AI edits have watermark signals at least as strong as this submission?”"
- The guarantee, Scenario 1 (p.6): "This method guarantees that, for independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) essays genuinely following permitted AI usage, Pr(û(s) ≤ α) ≤ α, regardless of the underlying distribution of watermark scores."
- Scenario 2, hierarchical (p.7): "Hierarchical conformal prediction extends the standard conformal framework to effectively handle such grouped data [Lee et al., 2023], assuming hierarchical exchangeability." Randomness named explicitly (p.8): "This probability accounts for randomness at two levels: how the assignment is designed, and how the students completed this assignment." Guarantee (p.8): "if new student follows the AI guideline, the probability of false positive rate satisfies Pr(uhier (s) ≤ α) ≤ α."
- Scenario 3, weighted (p.10): "Under the assumption of weighted exchangeability [Tibshirani et al., 2019, Angelopoulos et al., 2024], this method retains the guarantee as before." and "even with imperfect estimates of this likelihood ratio, the validity of the weighted conformal methods can still be guaranteed, with the coverage error rate controlled by the error of the estimate;"
- The null is adherence to the guideline, not human authorship (p.4): "This approach prioritizes controlling the false positive rate (FPR) of detection methods, defined as the probability of incorrectly rejecting the null hypothesis that a submission adheres to AI usage guidelines,"
- The watermark key is never mentioned: the only hit for the search term key is the word "Keywords" (p.1). The watermarking configuration is fixed throughout (p.33): "Both watermarking schemes use a context window size n = 4. For Green/Red List watermark, we set hyperparameters δ = 2 and γ = 0.5."

Reader's inference. The random objects are the essays (their watermark scores), assumed exchangeable or hierarchically / weighted exchangeable between calibration and test; the probability is marginal over the exchangeable draw of calibration and test essays. The watermark key is not a random object in the analysis: the same detection provider scores calibration and test essays, so whatever key was used is held fixed and the guarantee holds conditionally on it. In the terms of the research question this is a key-conditional null-text calibration, but the paper never frames it that way and never states that the key is fixed.

## Q2 Fixed key / per-key / conditional vs marginal

Observed.

- Motivation that raw watermark p-values are miscalibrated under permitted edits (p.3): "a naïve application—flagging any submission with watermark p-value below a fixed α threshold—can fail to maintain the expected false positive rate at α." with numbers "while only 4% of the human-written essays attain watermark p-values below the α = 0.05 threshold, 25.5% of the essays with permitted AI-grammar editing attain watermark p-values below the same threshold."
- Calibration as learning the null (p.4): "by using the historical data as a calibration set, we learn what “normal” looks like"
- The null is defined by the permitted edit (p.12): "The instructor-permitted prompt is defined as the “null” edit."
- Repetition is over calibration sampling, not keys (p.12): "at a target significance level of α = 0.05. Each experiment is repeated with five random seeds in sampling the calibration set." and (p.14): "each metric is averaged over the five repeated experiments with different random seeds."
- Variation across models and schemes is noted as a practical obstacle (p.19): "with the same model as the test set may not be feasible in practice, given the vast number of choices of language models available in the market." and (p.21): "Our comparative analysis across different AI models and watermarking scheme reveals noticeable variability in detection efficacy."
- Key dependence of false positives, a fixed or deployed key, per-key calibration, key screening, conditional versus marginal validity: not found (searched: key (only "Keywords" p.1), fixed (p.3 only), conditional (0), marginal (0), derandom (0), aggregat (p.27, a cited title), deployed (0), screening (0), Type I (0), per-key (0), seed (pp.12, 14 as above)).

Reader's inference. The paper is the clearest example among the five of calibrating a watermark detector against null texts scored with the deployed configuration, and it reports that the raw watermark p-value is not calibrated for its intended null (4% vs 25.5% at α = 0.05). It never asks whether that miscalibration or the conformal cure depends on the key, and all its "random seeds" refer to calibration-set sampling.

## Q3 Empirical FPR procedure

Observed.

- Data (p.10): "ETS TOEFL11 (ETS) Corpus [Blanchard et al., 2013], consisting of 8,884 essays by non-native speakers across eight writing prompts; (2) LOCNESS Corpus [Granger, 1998], including 385 essays by native speakers covering 273 unique topics." Models and schemes (p.11): "Phi-4-mini-instruct [Abdin et al., 2024] and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [Yang et al., 2024], applying two watermarking methods: Gumbel-Max [Aaronson and Kirchner, 2023] and Green/Red List [Kirchenbauer et al., 2023]."
- Level and calibration sizes (p.12): α = 0.05 as quoted in Q2; "Randomly hold out 200 essays, from which we sample ncal ∈ {30, 50, 200} essays for calibration." Test-set sizes per ETS writing prompt (Table 2, p.12): 1024, 962, 783, 887, 997, 492, 1076, 1063. Scenario 3 minority calibration sizes m ∈ {5, 15, 30} with all 385 LOCNESS essays (p.13).
- Metric (p.13): "False Positive Rate (FPR): The proportion of essays that are classified as outliers when they are following the permitted guidelines."
- Results versus nominal (p.15): "the standard conformal method effectively controls the FPR across AI-usage guidelines, maintaining it around or below the 5% significance level on average. When the number of calibration essays increases, the FPR shows a trend of converging towards the nominal 5% level,"
- Illustrative numbers: Figure 3 (p.7): "with only 4.69% falling below α = 0.05 (red line)—confirming the theoretical guarantee on false positive rate." Figure 4 (p.8): "Essays following guidelines show pvalues with only 4.32% falling below."
- Scenario 3, Table 3 (p.19; FPR in percent for m = 5, 15, 30; null edit 1 vs alternative 7): In Dist. Only 0.0, 0.0, 3.4; Comb. Unweighted 12.7, 12.7, 11.8; Comb. Weighted Quantile 6.4, 8.4, 6.6; Comb. Weighted Mean 9.7, 7.9, 6.7. Discussion (p.18): "Both weighted methods lowers the FPR compared to the Comb. Unweighted method, even though at times the FPR may not be as close to the nominal level of 5% as the In Dist. Only method," and (p.17): "When the calibration set size m is small, this method is useless when α is less than one divided by the 1/m (e.g., α = 0.05, m = 5 or 15)."
- Trade-off (p.17): "If more data are clustered together, the hierarchical method can still maintain valid FPR control, but at the cost of detection power."

Reader's inference. FPR is measured against null texts (permitted-edit essays) under one fixed watermark configuration per experiment, averaged over five calibration draws and eight writing prompts, and compared with the nominal 5%; the number of watermark keys is one per (model, scheme) and is never varied.

## Reader's inference

1. The guarantee is marginal over exchangeable essays and silent about the key; because the same deployed watermark configuration scores both calibration and test essays, the guarantee holds conditionally on that configuration. This is exactly the null-text calibration remedy named in the research question, applied with the key fixed, but the paper does not recognise the key as a source of randomness at all.
2. The paper's motivating observation (4% of human essays vs 25.5% of permitted-edit essays below α = 0.05) is about the wrong null (human text vs permitted edits), not about key dispersion, so it should not be cited as evidence of per-key miscalibration.
3. For the gate the paper is useful as (a) a precedent for conformal calibration of watermark p-values and (b) an empirical procedure (five calibration draws, eight prompts, nominal 5%) that can be adapted to vary the key instead.
4. Most decisive quotation: p.6, "Pr(û(s) ≤ α) ≤ α, regardless of the underlying distribution of watermark scores."
