## Q1 Datasets
- Observed: two layers of data. (i) Human arena votes: p.4 "We consider the instructions, pairwise completions, and accompanying human preference labels" [symbol y*_ij(x) ∈ {0, 0.5, 1}] "from LMArena 100K, a multilingual subset of Chatbot Arena [1]." Pool size M = 55 models; p.21: "The main experiments use Chatbot Arena battles drawn from a multilingual prompt pool, though the distribution is English-dominated (13,423 of ≈ 23,000 shared prompts, or ≈ 58%)." (ii) LLM-judge annotations of a subsample of those same battles by eight open-weight judges: p.4 "For each judge, we annotate approximately 25,000 candidate battle rows, sampled to spread model appearances across the M = 55 models in the LMArena 100K subset."
- Observed: replication corpora (Appendix D, pp.18–21): LMArena 140K (52-model held-out test pool) and ComparIA (French; 116-model pool). Per-language counts in Figure 5 (p.17) and Table 6 (p.23).
- Observed fields: instruction, two completions, human label y* ∈ {0, 0.5, 1}, judge per-criterion scores on six 1–10 criteria averaged to a scalar score difference s(x), swap-averaged over both presentation orders (pp.4, 15).
- Observed: the human Elo is the target, computed from the human votes themselves. p.4: "The target of inference is each model’s Elo rating on the fitted human leaderboard — an observable quantity from Chatbot Arena votes — not an unobserved latent skill parameter."
- Reader's inference: the human-vote BT fit is treated as the ground truth that LLM-derived Elo should reproduce; its own adequacy is never questioned.

## Q2 Models and ties
- Observed: standard L2-regularised BT fitted to ternary labels, with the Chatbot Arena tie convention. p.4: "We follow the Chatbot Arena convention [1] and split a tied battle into two rows of weight 0.5 (one per side); this is equivalent to plugging y = 0.5 directly into Eq. 1" (λ = 0.01, Elo = 1500 + (400/ln 10)θ; this pipeline is called Hard-Elo).
- Observed: Soft-Elo keeps the BT model and replaces the hard label by a calibrated soft target ỹ(x) = σ(β s(x)) with a per-judge temperature β fitted by MLE on human non-tie battles (Eq. 5–6, pp.6–7). p.3: "Departing from previous work, we leave the BT model unchanged and modify only its training targets, to account for the uncertainty estimated at the instruction level."
- Observed, ties: human ties are kept in the BT fit (weight-0.5 rows) but dropped from the temperature calibration, p.7: "Human ties are excluded because Eq. 6 calibrates a binary preference map." A judge verdict that flips under position swap is recorded as a tie (pp.4, 15). Appendix C (p.18) reports a sensitivity run fitting β on all battles with ties as 0.5, which roughly halves β and raises MAE by about 7 Elo.
- Observed: the related-work paragraph names tie- and covariance-aware and intransitive-preference models as alternatives but does not use them, p.3: "Recent extensions mostly modify the ranking model rather than the labels, including tie- and covariance-aware arena models [20], stable or annotator-aware Elo variants [21], methods for intransitive preferences [22], and non-parametric alternatives [23]."
- Observed: no Rao–Kupper or Davidson tie model (searched: Rao, Davidson — 0 hits).

## Q3 Adequacy tests and results
- Observed: no goodness-of-fit, lack-of-fit, likelihood-ratio, saturated-model, transitivity/cycle/triad or homogeneity test of the BT model against the human votes (searched: goodness 0, "lack of fit" 0, "likelihood ratio" 0, saturated 0, transitiv 5 hits all descriptive, cycle 1 hit p.30, triad 0, heterogene 1 hit about nonconformity scores, homogene 0, misspecif 0).
- Observed: the diagnostics that do exist concern the judge-vs-human discrepancy, not BT-vs-votes. (a) Scale distortion: p.5 "BT’s win-probability σ(θi − θj ) is systematically overconfident, since judge-side |θi − θj | exceeds its human counterpart and σ(·) saturates closer to 0 or 1 than the underlying preference data warrant." Table 1 (p.5): Hard-Elo held-out Elo MAE 27.5–63.4 across the eight judges, κ 0.147–0.231, Spearman ρ 0.868–0.976. (b) Structured residuals: p.6 "Figure 2 shows that Hard-Elo residuals are not exchangeable across model strength. Across all judges, the signed residual εi = EloLLM,i − EloHuman,i correlates positively with human Elo, with per-judge Pearson correlations from +0.49 to +0.90." (c) Bias decomposition, p.6: "Known battle-level biases, such as position, verbosity, and self-preference, are measurable in our data, but they do not by themselves explain the monotone residual ramp (Appendix F.1)." (d) Calibration of σ(β|s|) by ECE: 0.027–0.058 for seven judges, 0.102 for Qwen3.5-27B (Figure 15, Table 7, pp.26–27).
- Observed: the authors' own reading, p.6: "This points at the diagnosis underlying the section: the problem is not BT itself, but the hard targets it is fit to."
- Observed: the exchangeability assumption is framed as an empirical question but only for judge residuals, p.5: "Under exchangeability of calibration and test scores, this interval achieves marginal coverage of at least 1−α. Whether judge-derived Hard-Elo residuals satisfy that condition — and whether the resulting intervals are tight enough to be useful — is the question we take up in Section 4."
- Observed results: p.8 "Held-out Elo MAE falls by 39–73%, with mean MAE dropping from 45.9 to 17.9 Elo." p.9 "Table 3 shows the result: Soft-Elo reduces median split-conformal width by 39–70% while keeping empirical coverage near the 90% nominal target (88.6–97.9% for Hard-Elo; 92.1–96.4% for Soft-Elo)."
- Observed: Appendix J acknowledges, without testing, that BT may be misspecified for cyclic preferences. p.30: "Relation to Bradley–Terry assumptions. Soft-Elo changes the targets passed into Bradley–Terry, but it does not change the Bradley–Terry model class. The resulting leaderboard is still a scalar Elo projection of a potentially cyclic preference relation. Preference cycles, non-transitive comparisons," [p.31] "and model-pair-specific interactions therefore remain limitations of any BT-based leaderboard."
- Reader's inference: the paper diagnoses misfit of judge-derived labels to the human leaderboard; it does not ask whether the human leaderboard itself fits the human votes.

## Q4 Uncertainty and propagation
- Observed: two layers. Local: per-model bootstrap standard error of the LLM-derived Elo from resampling that model's battles (20 within-model resamples, p.9). Global: split conformal prediction on normalised residuals S_i = |ε_i|/SE_i across held-out models (Eq. 3–4, pp.4–5), p.9: "For each judge, we run five random model-level splits of the 55 held-out models," "using 27 models for conformal calibration and 28 for testing." Nominal 90% coverage; realised coverage and widths in Table 3 (p.9), Table 5 (p.20).
- Observed: the residual being covered is judge Elo minus human Elo; the human Elo carries no uncertainty of its own in the construction (p.4 defines ε_i = Elo_LLM,i − Elo_Human,i).
- Observed: the bootstrap ignores prompt-level dependence, p.30: "Finally, battle-level dependence remains because the same prompt can appear across many model pairs; our bootstrap resamples battles within each model and does not cluster by prompt."
- Observed: no propagation of BT misspecification (cycles, pair-specific effects, heterogeneity across language or prompt) into any interval; Appendix J (pp.30–31) lists these as remaining limitations.
- Reader's inference: the conformal layer absorbs judge–human disagreement empirically, which is a form of robustness to one misspecification (judge bias), but the human-vote BT fit is the fixed reference, so any lack of fit of BT to the votes is neither measured nor propagated.

## Q5 Limitations and future work
- p.9: "Two limitations remain. First, Soft-Elo uses only the uncertainty encoded in the judge’s score difference; it does not model epistemic uncertainty, hallucinated scores, prompt ambiguity, or judge instability. Second, the conformal guarantee is marginal and relies on exchangeability between calibration and future models."
- p.30 (Appendix J, sources of non-exchangeability): prompt-difficulty shift, model-family shift, temporal drift, and "Finally, battle-level dependence remains because the same prompt can appear across many model pairs; our bootstrap resamples battles within each model and does not cluster by prompt." Suggested extensions named there: prompt-block bootstraps, weighted conformal prediction, stratified conformal quantiles.
- pp.30–31 (Appendix J, "Relation to Bradley–Terry assumptions"): preference cycles, non-transitive comparisons and model-pair-specific interactions "remain limitations of any BT-based leaderboard."
- Observed: no explicit statement that adequacy testing of BT on human arena votes remains to be done; the closest is the Appendix J acknowledgement above, which names the misspecification but proposes no test.

## Reader's inference
- Relevance to the research question: this paper fits BT to public LMArena human votes and treats the result as the ground-truth leaderboard; it never tests BT's fit to those votes. The explicit admission in Appendix J that a BT leaderboard "is still a scalar Elo projection of a potentially cyclic preference relation" is the single most useful statement for a novelty claim: the authors recognise the misspecification and leave it untested.
- The data-handling details are a ready template for an adequacy study on the same public releases: tie splitting into two 0.5-weight rows, λ = 0.01 ridge, the Elo scale, the ~23,000 shared prompts, and the acknowledged failure to cluster by prompt.
- The within-model battle bootstrap plus conformal wrapper quantifies judge disagreement, not BT lack of fit; a sandwich or cluster-robust variance for the human-vote fit would be a different, complementary object.
