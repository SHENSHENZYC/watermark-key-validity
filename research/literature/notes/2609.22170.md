## Q1 Datasets
- Observed: the data are LLM forced-choice pairwise preferences, not human arena votes. Seven open-weight models (DeepSeek-R1 14B, GPT-OSS 20B, Gemma 3 12B, Gemma 4 31B, OLMo 3 7B, Qwen 2.5 14B, Qwen 3.6 35B; Table 3, p.16) × four item sets of 50 items each (Jokes, Moral Machine, Population, Half-lives; Table 1, p.3). p.3: "Each LLM sees every unordered item pair under four prompt variants and both presentation orders, with M samples per condition (8M attempted comparisons per pair). Human comparison counts vary by pair because they come from existing crowdsourced datasets."
- Observed: human crowdsourced pairwise comparisons are used only as an additional "Human" rater for Jokes (Simpson et al.) and Moral Machine (Awad et al.), restricted to the giant strongly connected component (Appendix A, p.15). p.6: "We apply the MBT model to 29 (LLM, dataset) pairs," (28 LLM–dataset pairs plus one human set appear in Table 2, p.6).
- Observed fields: ordered item pair shown, prompt variant, presentation order, random pseudo-identifiers, parsed forced-choice winner; aggregated to a win-count matrix a_ij (Algorithm 1, p.17; Appendix F, p.20).
- Observed: the word "arena" occurs only generically, p.9: "Judge ensembles, simple reward models, and arena leaderboards aggregate heterogeneous model behavior to a single ranking." and in two references.
- Reader's inference: nothing in this paper is fitted to Chatbot Arena or LMArena vote data.

## Q2 Models and ties
- Observed: (i) a non-parametric null for any random-utility model (Section 4); (ii) a noise-augmented Bradley–Terry model following Jerdee and Newman, p.5: "With probability α, a fair random comparison is made; otherwise, we follow the Bradley–Terry rules." giving p_ij = α/2 + (1−α)σ(θ_i − θ_j) (Eq. 2, p.6); (iii) its mixture extension, the noise-augmented mixture Bradley–Terry (MBT) model with K_max = 5 components, p.6: "To facilitate model fit, we place a Dirichlet process prior with concentration parameter γ on the mixture weights and implement it via a truncated stick-breaking representation." Priors: sum-to-zero Gaussian on scores, Beta(1.5, 1.5) on α_k (Appendix F, pp.20–22); HMC/NUTS in BlackJAX, 4 chains × 4,000 post-warm-up draws, Hungarian alignment for label switching (Appendix G, p.23).
- Observed, ties: choices are forced binary; p.16 "Unparseable outputs are discarded before constructing the pairwise win-count matrix." For the human Jokes data, p.15: "Ratings can be one of A, B, or X (“neither funnier”), and we keep only the definite ratings when constructing the preference matrix." The α "luck" parameter is the only device absorbing indifference; in the synthetic study it "controls the tie rate, and therefore how discriminating the pairwise comparisons are." (p.24).
- Observed: no Rao–Kupper or Davidson tie model (searched: Rao 0, Davidson 0). Note: "Singh and Davidov [46]" (p.4) is a different author (Ori Davidov) and concerns a cyclicality model, not the Davidson tie model.

## Q3 Adequacy tests and results
- Observed: yes, a formal non-parametric adequacy test against the whole random-utility class, applied to LLM (and two human) preference matrices. p.1: "We first show that observed inconsistencies cannot be explained by a single ordering under any monotone link function." p.4: "All random utility models, including Thurstone and Bradley–Terry, satisfy strong stochastic transitivity (SST): pik ≥ max(pij , pjk ) whenever pij , pjk ≥ 1/2." The statistic is the mean SST deficit D_SST over Borda-ordered triplets (Eq. 1, p.4). p.4: "We adapt work by Singh and Davidov [46] to build a non-parametric null that asks how large DSST should be under any random utility model, and compare the observed deficit against bootstrap samples from this null." Null: isotonic regression of p̂_ij on the Borda gap b_i − b_j anchored at g(0) = 1/2, binomial parametric bootstrap with B = 1000 (Appendix D, pp.17–18); p.18: "A small upper-tail probability indicates that the observed deficit is larger than what any random utility model would produce."
- Observed results: p.4 "We apply the test to all dataset pairs and reject the null hypothesis in nearly all cases at high significance." Controls for protocol artefacts, p.4: "In order to rule these out, we recompute the statistics within fixed prompt and presentation-order conditions." and p.5: "significantly intransitive (p < 0.01) across all model-data pairs." Summary, p.5: "The tests show that 1) there are significant levels of SST violation across all models and datasets and 2) in no case can those SST violations be attributed to any single set of ordinal preferences under any i.i.d. noise model."
- Observed, Table 4 (p.20; eight fixed prompt × order strata per model–dataset pair): "Excess is defined as the observed statistic minus the bootstrap mean under a matched Borda-link null fitted within the same stratum. SST uses mean positive triplet deficit; MST uses mean absolute multiplicative cycle residual." and "All fixed-stratum tests are significant at p ≤ .05; most attain the bootstrap floor p = .001." Median ΔSST excesses in the table range from 0.0002 (Qwen 3.6 35B, Population) to 0.0924, and every stratum-level minimum printed is positive. Appendix E (p.19) also defines WST, SST and MST excesses and an order-symmetrised matrix.
- Observed, model comparison: predictive, not likelihood-ratio. p.8: "We rule this out with 5-fold cross-validation across all four tasks and all LLMs." (item pairs held out; ΔMSE = MSE_BT − MSE_model, Eq. 5). p.8: "The MBT model matches or outperforms both baselines in the majority of cases." and "Gains are largest on H ALF - LIVESand M ORAL M ACHINE, where keff ≫ 1; on P OPULATIONand J OKES, where posteriors are near-unimodal, the advantage is small or absent." Effective component counts (Table 2, p.6; "Table 2 reports the posterior mean of keff and its 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.") range from 1.00 (e.g. Gemma 4 31B on Moral Machine) to 4.89 (DeepSeek-R1 14B on Moral Machine); Human on Moral Machine 2.57 [2.48, 2.67]. Posterior predictive calibration plot, p.28: "The MBT model lies closer to the diagonal than the single-component noise-augmented Bradley–Terry baseline, indicating better calibration of pairwise probabilities."
- Observed: per-item SST violation rates are reported for Population (p.26: 42% DeepSeek-R1 14B, 40% Qwen 2.5 14B, 12% Gemma 4 31B).
- Observed: no likelihood-ratio test against a saturated model, no deviance, no formal heterogeneity test across prompt variants beyond re-running the SST test within strata (searched: "likelihood ratio" 0, saturated 0, goodness 0, "lack of fit" 0, deviance 0).
- Reader's inference: this is the closest thing in the set to a formal specification test of BT-type models, but it is applied to LLM choice data on 50-item sets with dense repeated comparisons, a design very different from sparse, crowd-sourced arena votes.

## Q4 Uncertainty and propagation
- Observed: uncertainty is Bayesian posterior uncertainty on scores, mixture weights and luck parameters (credible intervals in Table 2; posterior SD as point size in Figures 8–9), with convergence gates, p.23: "A fit is deemed acceptable if all components satisfy R̂ < 1.01, ESS ≥ 100, and zero divergent transitions are observed." Table 2 flags three fits with poor HMC convergence.
- Observed: synthetic recovery study (Appendix H, pp.23–26) shows score recovery r = 1.00 at K_true = 1 and r = 0.95 at K_true = 5, with α underestimated at K_true = 5.
- Observed: no rank intervals, no leaderboard-level uncertainty, and no propagation of intransitivity into a rank uncertainty; the response to misspecification is to change the model (mixture) rather than to widen intervals.
- Reader's inference: the paper's answer to BT lack of fit is a richer generative model; it offers no recipe for honest uncertainty on a single-ordering leaderboard that is known to be misspecified.

## Reader's inference
- The paper demonstrates, with a bootstrap test against a non-parametric random-utility null, that single-ordering models (including BT) are rejected for LLM pairwise choices, and it shows a mixture-BT improves held-out prediction. This is strong evidence that the methodology for testing BT adequacy exists and is cheap (243 diagnostic jobs ≈ 1.1 CPU-hours, Table 5 p.30).
- It does not apply any of this to human arena votes, to sparse comparison graphs, or to rank uncertainty, so it leaves the research question open while supplying a directly reusable test (SST deficit with Borda-link isotonic null) and a competitor model (MBT) that an arena study could adopt as alternatives to a saturated pairwise model.
- Caveat for reuse: the test presumes dense repeated comparisons per pair; arena data have many pairs with few votes, so the bootstrap null and Borda proxy would need re-derivation or restriction to well-sampled sub-tournaments.
